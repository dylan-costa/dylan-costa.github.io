Elasticity of Demand
↑ Back to top
Goals Define and interpret elasticity of demand; understand what elasticity tells us about a product's demand function; understand what it means for demand to be elastic, inelastic and unit elastic; learn how to use elasticity of demand to maximize revenue.

How Sensitive Is Demand to Price?

Compare the demand for furniture (say, a couch) with the demand for gas (a gallon). What happens when the price goes up? In both cases demand goes down — but for gas we expect demand to drop much less, because people still need to drive. Elasticity is a number that measures how strongly demand reacts to a change in price.

Is a $10 price increase "a lot"? It depends on the starting price: $10 is a huge jump for a $5 item and barely noticeable for a $2000 couch. So instead of raw changes we compare percent changes:

\[ \text{percent change in price} = \frac{\Delta p}{p}, \qquad \text{percent change in demand} = \frac{\Delta x}{x}. \]

The idea is to look at the ratio of the percent change in demand to the percent change in price. Since one goes up when the other goes down, this ratio is negative, so we put a minus sign in front to make it positive:

\[ -\frac{\ \Delta x / x\ }{\Delta p / p} = -\frac{\Delta x}{x}\cdot\frac{p}{\Delta p} = -\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta p}\cdot\frac{p}{x}. \]

When the change in price is very small, the difference quotient \(\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta p}\) becomes the derivative \(\frac{dx}{dp}\). This gives the definition.

Definition (Elasticity of Demand) If the demand equation is given by \(x = f(p)\), where \(p\) is the price per item and \(x\) is the number of items sold at that price, then the elasticity of demand is \[ E(p) = \frac{-p}{x}\cdot\frac{dx}{dp}. \]
Remark Demand goes down as price goes up, so \(\frac{dx}{dp}\) is negative. The minus sign in the formula cancels that out, so \(E(p)\) is a positive number. Also notice that the formula needs demand written as \(x = \) (something in \(p\)). If you are given \(p\) in terms of \(x\), solve for \(x\) first.
Definition (Elastic, Inelastic, Unit Elastic)
  • If the percent change in demand is less than the percent change in price, then \(E(p) < 1\) and we say demand is inelastic.
  • If the percent change in demand is more than the percent change in price, then \(E(p) > 1\) and we say demand is elastic.
  • If the percent change in demand is equal to the percent change in price, then \(E(p) = 1\) and we say demand is unit elastic.

Inelastic demand means changing the price doesn't have a huge impact on demand: think medication, milk, or baby formula — things people need no matter what. Elastic demand means demand reacts strongly to price: think restaurant meals, or anything with lots of substitutes.

Computing Elasticity of Demand
  1. Write demand in the form \(x = f(p)\) (solve for \(x\) if necessary).
  2. Find \(\dfrac{dx}{dp}\).
  3. Plug into \(E(p) = -\dfrac{dx}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{x}\), replace \(x\) by its formula in \(p\), and simplify.
Example The demand equation for a 20 oz soda is \(p = 3-2x\), where \(p\) is the price and \(x\) is the number sold (in hundreds) in one week. Find the elasticity of demand at \(p = 1.25,\ 1.50,\ 2\). Is demand elastic, inelastic or unit elastic in each case?
1. Solve for \(x\).
\[ p = 3-2x \;\Longrightarrow\; p - 3 = -2x \;\Longrightarrow\; x = -\tfrac12 p + \tfrac32. \]
2. Differentiate.
\[ \frac{dx}{dp} = -\frac12. \]
3. Plug in and simplify.
\[ E(p) = -\left(-\frac12\right)\cdot\frac{p}{-\frac12p+\frac32} = \frac{p}{2\left(-\frac12p+\frac32\right)} = \frac{p}{3-p}. \]

Now evaluate:

\[ E(1.25) = \frac{1.25}{1.75} = \frac57 \approx 0.714, \qquad E(1.5) = \frac{1.5}{1.5} = 1, \qquad E(2) = \frac{2}{1} = 2. \]

So demand is inelastic at \(p = 1.25\), unit elastic at \(p = 1.50\), and elastic at \(p = 2\).

Interpreting Elasticity

In general, think of elasticity as

\[ E(p) = \frac{\%\text{ decrease in demand}}{\%\text{ increase in price}}. \]
Example Suppose \(E(4) = \tfrac35\). What does this mean? Does increasing the price increase or decrease revenue?

\(E(4) = \dfrac{3\%\text{ decrease in demand}}{5\%\text{ increase in price}}\): when the price is $4, a 5% increase in price leads to (about) a 3% decrease in demand. Demand falls by a smaller percentage than price rises, so raising the price increases revenue.

Now suppose instead \(E(4) = 2 = \dfrac{2\%\text{ decrease in demand}}{1\%\text{ increase in price}}\). When the price is $4, a 1% increase in price leads to a 2% decrease in demand. We lose more customers than we gain in price, so raising the price decreases revenue.

Elasticity and Revenue
  • If \(E(p) > 1\) (elastic), increasing the price decreases revenue.
  • If \(E(p) < 1\) (inelastic), increasing the price increases revenue.
  • If \(E(p) = 1\) (unit elastic), revenue is maximized.
Remark (Why This Works) Revenue is \(R = p\cdot x\), where \(x\) depends on \(p\). By the product rule, \[ \frac{dR}{dp} = x + p\frac{dx}{dp} = x\left(1 + \frac{p}{x}\frac{dx}{dp}\right) = x\bigl(1 - E(p)\bigr). \] Since \(x > 0\), revenue is increasing when \(E < 1\), decreasing when \(E > 1\), and has a critical point exactly when \(E = 1\).
Example (Maximizing Revenue) The demand for a product is \(x = (36-2p)^2\), where \(p\) is the price and \(x\) is the number of items sold, \(0\le p\le 18\). What should be charged to maximize revenue?

To maximize revenue, set \(E = 1\). By the chain rule,

\[ \frac{dx}{dp} = 2(36-2p)\cdot(-2) = -4(36-2p). \]

So

\[ E(p) = -\bigl(-4(36-2p)\bigr)\cdot\frac{p}{(36-2p)^2} = \frac{4p\,\cancel{(36-2p)}}{(36-2p)^{\cancel{2}}} = \frac{4p}{36-2p}. \]

Set \(E(p) = 1\):

\[ \frac{4p}{36-2p} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4p = 36 - 2p \;\Longrightarrow\; 6p = 36 \;\Longrightarrow\; p = 6. \]

Revenue is maximized at a price of $6. Check: graphing \(R(p) = p(36-2p)^2\) on \([0,18]\) shows a peak at \((6, 3456)\).

Example A small grocery store makes their own candy bars and finds \(E(p) = \dfrac{p}{5-p}\). How much should they charge to maximize revenue? \[ \frac{p}{5-p} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; p = 5 - p \;\Longrightarrow\; 2p = 5 \;\Longrightarrow\; p = 2.5. \]

Revenue is maximized when \(p = \)$2.50.

Estimating Changes in Demand

Rearranging \(E = \dfrac{\%\text{ decrease in demand}}{\%\text{ increase in price}}\) gives a quick estimate:

\[ \%\text{ decrease in demand} \approx E(p)\cdot(\%\text{ increase in price}). \]
Example The price of season tickets is $40 and \(E(40) = 1.75\). If the price increases by $4, what change do we expect in demand? If demand was 1000, what would the new demand be?

Going from $40 to $44 is an increase of \(\frac{4}{40} = 0.10\), i.e. a 10% increase in price. Then

\[ \%\text{ decrease in demand} \approx 1.75 \times 10\% = 17.5\%. \]

New demand \(\approx 1000 - (0.175)(1000) = 1000 - 175 = 825\) tickets.

Example Demand is given by \(x(p) = 1200 - 6p^2\). Find \(x(10)\) and \(E(10)\). Use these to estimate \(x(11)\), then compare with the actual value.
1. Plug \(p = 10\) into the demand equation.
\[ x(10) = 1200 - 6(100) = 600. \]
2. Find \(E(p)\) first, then plug in \(p = 10\).
\[ \frac{dx}{dp} = -12p, \qquad E(p) = -(-12p)\cdot\frac{p}{1200-6p^2} = \frac{12p^2}{1200-6p^2}, \qquad E(10) = \frac{1200}{600} = 2. \]
3. Interpret and estimate.

When the price is $10, a 1% increase in price leads to about a 2% decrease in demand. Going from \(p = 10\) to \(p = 11\) is a \(\frac{1}{10} = 10\%\) increase in price, so we expect about a \(20\%\) decrease in demand:

\[ x(11) \approx 600 - (0.20)(600) = 600 - 120 = 480. \]

Check: the actual value is \(x(11) = 1200 - 6(121) = 474\). Pretty close!

Goals Find and use the linearization of a function to approximate the function near a point; connect the idea of linear approximation to the idea of the cost (or value) of the "next" item.

Tangent Lines as Approximations

Recall how to find the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = a\). We need a point and a slope:

  • Point: \((a, f(a))\).
  • Slope: \(f'(a)\), the derivative at \(x = a\).

Then point-slope form gives \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x-a)\).

Example (Warm-up) Let \(f(x) = \sqrt x\). Find the tangent line at \(x = 4\) and at \(x = 9\).

First, \(f'(x) = \tfrac12x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\).

  • At \(x = 4\): point \((4, f(4)) = (4,2)\), slope \(f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt4} = \frac14\). Tangent line: \(y - 2 = \tfrac14(x-4)\), i.e. \(y = \tfrac14(x-4)+2\).
  • At \(x = 9\): point \((9, f(9)) = (9,3)\), slope \(f'(9) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt9} = \frac16\). Tangent line: \(y - 3 = \tfrac16(x-9)\), i.e. \(y = \tfrac16(x-9)+3\).

Near the point of tangency, the curve and the tangent line are almost indistinguishable. So the tangent line gives an approximation of the function near \(x = a\).

Definition (Linearization) The linearization (or linear approximation) of \(f\) at \(x = a\) is the tangent line at \(x=a\): \[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a). \] For \(x\) near \(a\), we have \(f(x)\approx L(x)\).

Example Find the linear approximation of \(f(x) = (1+x)^4\) at \(x = 0\). Use it to approximate \((1.02)^4\), and compare with the actual value.

Point: \((0, f(0)) = (0,1)\). Slope: by the chain rule \(f'(x) = 4(1+x)^3\cdot 1\), so \(f'(0) = 4\).

\[ y - 1 = 4(x-0) \;\Longrightarrow\; L(x) = 4x+1. \]

Notice that \((1.02)^4 = f(0.02)\), and \(0.02\) is close to \(0\). So

\[ (1.02)^4 = f(0.02)\approx L(0.02) = 4(0.02)+1 = 1.08. \]

The actual value is \((1.02)^4 = 1.08243216\dots\), very close.

Linear Approximation and the "Next" Item

Recall that the marginal cost \(C'(x)\) approximates the cost of producing one more item. Linear approximation is the same idea written as a line.

Example Let \(C(x) = x^2+4x+3\).

(a) Find the linearization of the cost function at \(x = 10\).

\[ C(10) = 100+40+3 = 143, \qquad C'(x) = 2x+4, \qquad C'(10) = 24. \] \[ y - 143 = 24(x-10) \;\Longrightarrow\; L(x) = 24(x-10)+143. \]

(b) Once we have made ten items, what is the approximate cost of the next item?

\(C'(10) = 24\): after making 10 items, the next item costs approximately $24. (The exact cost of the 11th item is \(C(11)-C(10) = 168 - 143 = \$25\).)

(c) Compare the linear approximation with the "cost of the next item" idea, for example by estimating the cost of 13 items.

The first ten items cost $143, and each of the next \(13-10 = 3\) items costs about $24:

\[ C(13)\approx 143 + 24(13-10) = 215 = L(13). \]

The two ideas give exactly the same computation. (Actual: \(C(13) = 224\).)

Example Assume \(C(20) = 53\) and \(C'(20) = 2\). Approximate \(C(22)\) two ways.
1. "Cost of the next item."

It costs $53 to make 20 items. The 21st costs about $2 and the 22nd costs about $2:

\[ C(22)\approx 53 + 2 + 2 = 53 + 2\cdot(22-20) = \$57. \]
2. Linear approximation.

Point \((20,53)\), slope \(2\): \(L(x) = 53 + 2(x-20)\), so \(L(22) = 53 + 2(2) = 57\). Same answer.

Example (Exam 3 Review) The cost in dollars to produce \(x\) tons of a product is \(C(x) = \sqrt{2x^2+25}\).

Marginal cost: by the chain rule,

\[ C'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2x^2+25}}\cdot 4x = \frac{2x}{\sqrt{2x^2+25}}. \]

At \(x = 10\): \(C(10) = \sqrt{225} = 15\) and \(C'(10) = \frac{20}{15} = \frac43\). So after producing 10 tons, the next ton costs about \(\$1.33\).

Linear approximation at 10: \(L(x) = 15 + \tfrac43(x-10)\), so the cost to produce 11 tons is about \(L(11) = 15 + \tfrac43 \approx \$16.33\) — the cost of 10 tons plus the marginal cost of one more. (Actual: \(C(11) = \sqrt{267}\approx 16.34\).)

Absolute Extrema
↑ Back to top
Goals Define absolute extrema; learn how to find critical points of a function; use critical points to find absolute extrema.

Biggest and Smallest Values

We often want to find the biggest and smallest values of a function: maximize revenue, maximize profit, minimize cost. Some vocabulary:

  • maxima is the plural of maximum, minima is the plural of minimum;
  • extrema (plural of extremum) means both minima and maxima.
Example
  • \(f(x) = x^2\) (on all real numbers) has a smallest value of \(0\) at \(x = 0\), but no largest value: the parabola keeps going up forever.
  • \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x+1}\) on \([-3,-1)\cup(-1,2]\) has no largest or smallest value: near the vertical asymptote \(x=-1\) the outputs blow up to \(\infty\) on one side and \(-\infty\) on the other.
Definition (Absolute Extrema) Let \(f\) be a function defined over an interval \(I\) and let \(c\) be in \(I\). We say \(f\) has an absolute maximum on \(I\) at \(c\) if \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(I\). We say \(f\) has an absolute minimum on \(I\) at \(c\) if \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(I\). If \(f\) has an absolute maximum or an absolute minimum on \(I\) at \(c\), we say \(f\) has an absolute extremum on \(I\) at \(c\).

In words: the absolute maximum is the single highest output over the whole interval, and the absolute minimum is the single lowest. A maximum value can happen at more than one \(x\): if a graph reaches a height of 10 at two different places \(x=c\) and \(x=d\) and never goes higher, the absolute max is 10, occurring at both \(x=c\) and \(x=d\).

When Are Absolute Extrema Guaranteed?

Extreme Value Theorem If \(f\) is a continuous function over the closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\), then there is a point in \([a,b]\) at which \(f\) has an absolute maximum over \([a,b]\) and there is a point in \([a,b]\) at which \(f\) has an absolute minimum over \([a,b]\).

Both conditions matter:

  • Closed interval. A line segment going up from an open circle at \((-1,1)\) to an open circle at \((2,3)\) has no max and no min: it gets arbitrarily close to 3 and to 1 but never reaches either, because the endpoints are not included.
  • Continuous. A graph with a jump can "escape" its highest point through the jump: the top of the jump is an open circle, so there is no max.
  • If the conditions fail, extrema might still exist by luck — the theorem just doesn't promise them. Even a constant function on \([a,b]\) satisfies the theorem: \(f(x) = 3\) has max 3 and min 3.

Critical Points

Now we know which functions have absolute extrema. Where (at which \(x\)-values) can they be located? Only two kinds of places:

  • at the endpoints of the interval;
  • at points where the derivative is zero (a smooth hilltop or valley, with a flat tangent line) or undefined (a sharp corner, like the bottom of a "V").
Definition (Critical Point) An \(x\)-value \(c\) in the domain of \(f\) where the derivative is zero or undefined is called a critical point (or critical value) of \(f\).
Example Find the critical points of \(f(x) = x^2-6x+14\). \[ f'(x) = 2x - 6 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3. \]

The derivative is defined everywhere, so the only critical point is \(x = 3\).

Finding Absolute Extrema on a Closed Interval

Closed Interval Method: Absolute Max and Min of \(f\) on \([a,b]\)
  1. Find \(f'(x)\).
  2. Set \(f'(x) = 0\) (and find where \(f'\) is undefined) and solve for \(x\) to find the critical points.
  3. Make a table with \(x = a\), \(x = b\), and every critical point between \(a\) and \(b\).
  4. Find the original \(f(x)\) value for each \(x\)-value.
  5. The biggest output is the absolute max, and the smallest is the absolute min.
Example Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x) = x^2-6x+14\) on \([2,5]\).

From before, the only critical point is \(x = 3\), which lies in \([2,5]\). Check it along with the endpoints \(x = 2\) and \(x = 5\), plugging into the original function:

\(x\)\(f(x) = x^2-6x+14\)
2\(4-12+14 = 6\)
3\(9-18+14 = 5\)min
5\(25-30+14 = 9\)max

The absolute max is 9 (at \(x = 5\)) and the absolute min is 5 (at \(x=3\)).

Example Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^3}{3}+\dfrac{x^2}{2}-6x+2\) on \([0,4]\). (The square brackets mean the interval is closed.) \[ f'(x) = \tfrac13\cdot 3x^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 2x - 6 = x^2+x-6 = (x-2)(x+3) = 0. \]

So \(x = 2\) or \(x = -3\). But \(-3\) is not between 0 and 4, so we throw it out.

\(x\)\(f(x)\)

Intervals That Are Not Closed

If the interval is not closed, the Extreme Value Theorem doesn't apply, but we can still try:

  • Treat the interval as if it were closed and make the same table.
  • If an absolute max (or min) occurs only at an endpoint which is not included, then there is no absolute max (or min).
Example Find the absolute extrema of \(f(x) = 4x^2-16x+5\) on \([1,4)\). \[ f'(x) = 8x - 16 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 2. \]
\(x\)\(f(x)\)
1\(4-16+5 = -7\)
2\(16-32+5 = -11\)absolute min
4 (not included)\(64-64+5 = 5\)would be the max, but \(x=4\) is not included

The absolute min is \(-11\) at \(x=2\). There is no absolute max: the function gets closer and closer to 5 as \(x\to4\), but never reaches it.

Both are in \([0,3]\) (and \(x=0\) is also an endpoint). Make the table:

What Are Local Extrema?

An absolute max is the highest point overall. A local (or relative) max is a point that is the highest compared to everyone nearby — the top of a hill, even if there is a taller hill somewhere else. Similarly a local min is the bottom of a valley.

Definition (Local Extrema) A function \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\) if there exists an open interval \(I\) containing \(c\) such that \(I\) is contained in the domain of \(f\) and \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(I\). A function \(f\) has a local minimum at \(c\) if there exists an open interval \(I\) containing \(c\) such that \(I\) is contained in the domain of \(f\) and \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(I\). A function \(f\) has a local extremum at \(c\) if \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\) or a local minimum at \(c\).
Remark
  • Because the definition needs an open interval around \(c\) inside the domain, there must be room on both sides of \(c\). On a flat (constant) piece of a graph, every point is both a local min and a local max.
  • A graph can have several local maxima of different heights; the absolute max (if it exists) is the biggest value overall.

Where Can Local Extrema Occur?

Local Extrema Occur at Critical Points If \(f\) has a local extremum at \(c\), then \(c\) is a critical point: either \(f'(c) = 0\) or \(f'(c)\) is undefined.
Remark (The Converse Is False) Just because \(f'(c) = 0\) doesn't mean we must have a local min or max. Take \(f(x) = x^3\): \[ f'(x) = 3x^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 0. \] So \(x = 0\) is a critical point. But the graph of \(x^3\) just flattens out for a moment at the origin and keeps going up — it is neither a min nor a max. Critical points are only the candidates.
Example What are the critical points of \(f(x) = e^x(x-3)^{2/3}\)?

By the product rule and the chain rule,

\[ f'(x) = e^x\cdot\tfrac23(x-3)^{-1/3}\cdot(1) + e^x(x-3)^{2/3} = e^x\left[\frac{2}{3(x-3)^{1/3}} + (x-3)^{2/3}\right]. \]

Where \(f'\) is undefined: the fraction has \((x-3)^{1/3}\) in the denominator, which is zero when \(x = 3\). Since \(f(3) = 0\) is defined, \(x = 3\) is a critical point.

Where \(f' = 0\): since \(e^x\) is never zero, the bracket must be zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{2}{3(x-3)^{1/3}} + (x-3)^{2/3} &= 0\\ \frac{2}{3(x-3)^{1/3}} &= -(x-3)^{2/3}\\ 2 &= -3(x-3)^{2/3}(x-3)^{1/3} \qquad\text{(multiply both sides by } 3(x-3)^{1/3})\\ 2 &= -3(x-3)\\ 2 &= -3x + 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; -7 = -3x \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \tfrac73. \end{aligned} \]

The critical points are \(x = 3\) and \(x = \tfrac73\).

Now we want to know: how can we tell whether these are local mins or local maxes (or neither)? That is exactly what the first derivative test, in the next section, does.

What Does the Derivative Tell Us?

The derivative \(f'(x)\) is the slope of the tangent line. Where the tangent line slopes upward (positive slope), the graph is going up; where it slopes downward (negative slope), the graph is going down.

Concavity

An increasing graph can rise in two different ways. If its slope is increasing (slopes like 0, 1, 2, 3, … getting steeper and steeper), the graph bends upward like a cup: concave up. If its slope is decreasing (slopes like 4, 2, 0, \(-2\), \(-4\), …), the graph bends downward like a frown: concave down.

Definition (Concavity) Let \(f\) be a function that is differentiable over an open interval \(I\). If \(f'\) is increasing over \(I\), we say \(f\) is concave up over \(I\). If \(f'\) is decreasing over \(I\), we say \(f\) is concave down over \(I\).
Remark (Be Careful) This definition says \(f'\) is increasing/decreasing, not \(f\). Increasing/decreasing and concavity are separate questions, and all four combinations happen:
Concave up \(\cup\)Concave down \(\cap\)
Increasingrising, getting steeperrising, leveling off
Decreasingfalling, leveling offfalling, getting steeper

Limits as \(x\to\pm\infty\)

A limit at infinity asks: what happens to \(f(x)\) as \(x\) gets larger and larger without bound (\(x\to\infty\)), or more and more negative (\(x\to-\infty\))? Two basic examples:

\[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x = 0, \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}x^2 = \infty \ \ (\text{grows without bound, so the limit does not exist as a number}). \]

Idea: a fixed number divided by something huge is tiny: \(\dfrac{\text{constant}}{\text{huge}}\to 0\). With "more \(x\)" on the bottom, as \(x\to\infty\) the fraction goes to 0.

What about \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+4x}{5x^2-3x}\)? Plugging in "\(\infty\)" gives \(\frac{\infty}{\infty-\infty}\), which is meaningless. Two ways to proceed:

Method 2: Algebraically

Technique for Limits at Infinity of Rational Functions
Divide each term (top and bottom) by \(x^n\), where \(n\) is the highest power in the denominator. Then use \(\dfrac{\text{constant}}{x^k}\to 0\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\).
Example The highest power in the denominator is \(x^2\): \[ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{3x^2+4x}{5x^2-3x} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\frac{3x^2}{x^2}+\frac{4x}{x^2}}{\frac{5x^2}{x^2}-\frac{3x}{x^2}} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{3+\frac4x}{5-\frac3x} = \frac{3+0}{5-0} = \frac35 = 0.6. \]
Example Divide each term by \(x\) (the highest power in the denominator): \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^2+4x}{3x+1} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\frac{x^2}{x}+\frac{4x}{x}}{\frac{3x}{x}+\frac1x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x+4}{3+\frac1x} = \infty, \]

since the top grows without bound while the bottom approaches 3. Similarly \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{x+4}{3+\frac1x} = -\infty\).

Example Divide each term by \(x^4\): \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^3+6x}{4x^4+5} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\frac3x+\frac{6}{x^3}}{4+\frac{5}{x^4}} = \frac{0+0}{4+0} = 0. \]

Horizontal Asymptotes

Definition (Horizontal Asymptote) The graph of \(f(x)\) has a horizontal asymptote at \(y = B\) if either \[ \lim_{x\to\infty}f(x) = B \qquad\text{or}\qquad \lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x) = B \] (or both).
Remark
  • A function can have one, two, or no horizontal asymptotes (a different one on each end is allowed).
  • Unlike vertical asymptotes, a function can cross its horizontal asymptote. The asymptote only describes the far left and far right of the graph.

So in the examples above: \(y = \tfrac35\) is a horizontal asymptote of \(\frac{3x^2+4x}{5x^2-3x}\); \(\frac{x^2+4x}{3x+1}\) has no horizontal asymptote; and \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote of \(\frac{3x^3+6x}{4x^4+5}\). For rational functions there is a shortcut, remembered as "BOBO BOTN EATS DC":

MnemonicMeaningHorizontal asymptote
BOBOBigger On BottomO: \(y = 0\)
BOTNBigger On TopNone
EATS DCExponents Are The SameDivide the leading Coefficients

("Bigger" refers to the highest power of \(x\).) The shortcut is a good check, but you should be able to justify the answer with the algebraic technique.

Example Use limits to determine if each function has a horizontal asymptote.
  • \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2+4x}{3x^2+2}\): dividing by \(x^2\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1+\frac4x}{3+\frac{2}{x^2}} = \frac13\). So \(y = \tfrac13\) is a horizontal asymptote.
  • \(g(x) = \dfrac{x^2+3x}{x-5}\): dividing by \(x\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x+3}{1-\frac5x} = \pm\infty\). No horizontal asymptote.
  • \(h(x) = \dfrac{x^3+6x}{x^4-6x}\): dividing by \(x^4\), \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\frac1x+\frac{6}{x^3}}{1-\frac{6}{x^3}} = \frac01 = 0\). So \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote.

Putting It All Together: Sketching Graphs

Drawing the Graph of a Function
  1. Determine the domain of the function.
  2. Locate the \(x\)- and \(y\)-intercepts.
  3. Evaluate \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)\) to determine the end behavior. If either limit is a finite number \(L\), then \(y = L\) is a horizontal asymptote.
  4. Determine whether \(f\) has any vertical asymptotes.
  5. Calculate \(f'\). Find all critical points and determine the intervals where \(f\) is increasing and decreasing. Determine any local extrema.
  6. Calculate \(f''\). Determine the intervals where \(f\) is concave up and concave down, and find any inflection points.
Example Use all the steps to sketch a graph of \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{1-x^2}\).
1. Domain.

\(1 - x^2 = (1+x)(1-x)\ne 0\), so \(x\ne\pm1\). Domain: \((-\infty,-1)\cup(-1,1)\cup(1,\infty)\).

2. Intercepts.

\(y\)-intercept: \(f(0) = \frac01 = 0\). \(x\)-intercepts: \(\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = 0\) means \(x^2 = 0\), so \(x = 0\). The only intercept is the origin.

3. End behavior.
\[ \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{\frac{x^2}{x^2}}{\frac{1}{x^2}-\frac{x^2}{x^2}} = \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{\frac1{x^2}-1} = \frac{1}{0-1} = -1. \]

Horizontal asymptote at \(y = -1\).

4. Vertical asymptotes.

At \(x = \pm1\) the top is \(1\) and the bottom is \(0\) ("\(\frac10\)"), so the function blows up. Checking signs of the bottom just to each side:

\[ \lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = +\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = -\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to-1^-}\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = -\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to-1^+}\frac{x^2}{1-x^2} = +\infty. \]

Vertical asymptotes at \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\).

5. Increasing/decreasing.
\[ f'(x) = \frac{(1-x^2)(2x) - x^2(-2x)}{(1-x^2)^2} = \frac{2x - 2x^3 + 2x^3}{(1-x^2)^2} = \frac{2x}{(1-x^2)^2}. \]

Critical point: \(x = 0\). (\(x = \pm1\) are not in the domain but still go on the number line.) The denominator is always positive, so the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(2x\):

6. Concavity.

Quotient rule again (the chain rule gives \(\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^2)^2 = 2(1-x^2)(-2x)\)):

\[ f''(x) = \frac{(1-x^2)^2(2) - 2x\cdot 2(1-x^2)(-2x)}{(1-x^2)^4} = \frac{(1-x^2)\bigl[2(1-x^2) + 8x^2\bigr]}{(1-x^2)^4} = \frac{2+6x^2}{(1-x^2)^3}. \]

The top \(2+6x^2\) is never zero (it is always positive). The bottom is zero at \(x = \pm1\). So the sign of \(f''\) is the sign of \((1-x^2)^3\), which is the sign of \(1-x^2\):

Applied Optimization
↑ Back to top
Goals Set up and solve optimization problems.

The Basic Idea

For a function of one variable, the min or max can only occur at critical points (where the first derivative is 0 or undefined) or at endpoints. But word problems often give us:

  • a function of two or more variables that we want to maximize or minimize — the objective function; and
  • extra information that relates the variables (an equation set equal to a number) — the constraint.

So we use the constraint to reduce the objective function to a single variable, then take the derivative and set it equal to 0.

Basic Steps for Optimization
  1. Variables: draw a picture and define your variables.
  2. Objective: determine the objective function — the function you want to minimize or maximize.
  3. Constraint: find any constraints, i.e. equations that relate the variables.
  4. One variable + domain: use the constraint to reduce the objective function to one variable, and find the domain of this new function.
  5. Derivative: find the derivative of the single-variable function and set it equal to 0 (and find where it's undefined) to get the critical points.
  6. Check! Test that you really have a min/max (a table with the endpoints, or a sign chart).

Example (Minimizing Cost) A company wants to produce a sturdy open-top rectangular container with a square base whose volume is 128 ft³. The sides cost $2 per square foot and the bottom costs $6 per square foot. Find the dimensions that minimize cost.

Variables: let \(x\) be the side length of the square base and \(y\) the height.

Objective: minimize cost.

\[ C = (\text{cost per ft}^2)(\text{area of bottom}) + (\text{cost per ft}^2)(\text{area of a side})(\#\text{ sides}) = 6x^2 + 2(xy)(4) = 6x^2+8xy. \]

Constraint: \(V = x^2y = 128\), so \(y = \dfrac{128}{x^2}\).

One variable + domain:

\[ C = 6x^2 + 8x\cdot\frac{128}{x^2} = 6x^2 + \frac{1024}{x}, \qquad x > 0. \]

Here \(x > 0\) (if \(x = 0\) the volume would be 0), but there is no upper bound, so we can't use the closed interval table. Instead we use a sign chart.

Derivative:

\[ C'(x) = 12x - \frac{1024}{x^2} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 12x = \frac{1024}{x^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 12x^3 = 1024 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^3 = \frac{256}{3} \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \sqrt[3]{\frac{256}{3}}\approx 4.40. \]

(\(C'\) is undefined at \(x = 0\), but that is not in the domain.)

\[ y = \frac{128}{x^2} = \frac{128}{(256/3)^{2/3}}\approx 6.60. \]

The cheapest container has a base about 4.40 ft by 4.40 ft and a height of about 6.60 ft (costing about $348.89).
