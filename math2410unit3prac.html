Problem 1 Easy

Using the transform table and linearity, find \[ \mathcal{L}\{6 - 4t^3 + 2e^{5t}\}. \]

Solution:

By linearity, we can transform each term separately and add the results, so there is no need to touch the definition at all here — we just need three entries from the transform table.

For the constant term, \(\mathcal{L}\{k\}=\dfrac{k}{s}\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{6\}=\dfrac{6}{s}\). For the power of \(t\), \(\mathcal{L}\{t^n\}=\dfrac{n!}{s^{n+1}}\) with \(n=3\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{t^3\}=\dfrac{3!}{s^4}=\dfrac{6}{s^4}\), and hence \(\mathcal{L}\{4t^3\}=\dfrac{24}{s^4}\). For the exponential term, \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}=\dfrac{1}{s-a}\) with \(a=5\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{2e^{5t}\}=\dfrac{2}{s-5}\).

Adding these three pieces together (with the correct signs) gives

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{6-4t^3+2e^{5t}\} = \frac{6}{s}-\frac{24}{s^4}+\frac{2}{s-5}.} \]

Problem 2 Easy

Prove directly from the definition that \[ \mathcal{L}\{e^{2t}\} = \frac{1}{s-2}, \qquad s>2. \]

Solution:

"Directly from the definition" means we go back to the defining improper integral rather than quoting the table entry. Starting from the definition,

\[ \mathcal{L}\{e^{2t}\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}e^{2t}\,dt. \]

Since the two exponentials share the same base, we can combine them into a single exponential before integrating:

\[ \mathcal{L}\{e^{2t}\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-(s-2)t}\,dt. \]

This is now the same basic exponential integral that appears throughout Section 7.1, so we antidifferentiate and evaluate the (improper) limits of integration:

\[ \int_0^\infty e^{-(s-2)t}\,dt = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac{1}{s-2}e^{-(s-2)t}\right]_0^b = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac{e^{-(s-2)b}}{s-2}+\frac{1}{s-2}\right). \]

Provided \(s-2>0\), i.e. \(s>2\), the term \(e^{-(s-2)b}\to0\) as \(b\to\infty\), and the limit reduces to just the second term. So

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{e^{2t}\} = \frac{1}{s-2}, \qquad s>2.} \]

Problem 3 Medium

Prove directly from the definition that \[ \mathcal{L}\{2t\} = \frac{2}{s^2}, \qquad s>0. \]

Solution:

By linearity, \(\mathcal{L}\{2t\}=2\,\mathcal{L}\{t\}\), so it suffices to evaluate \(\mathcal{L}\{t\}\) from the definition — this mirrors the \(\mathcal{L}\{t\}\) example already worked out in the Section 7.1 notes.

\[ \mathcal{L}\{t\} = \int_0^\infty te^{-st}\,dt. \]

Step 1 (Integrate by parts).

Since the integrand is a product of \(t\) and \(e^{-st}\), we use integration by parts with \(u=t\), \(dv=e^{-st}\,dt\), so \(du=dt\), \(v=-\frac{1}{s}e^{-st}\):

\[ \int te^{-st}\,dt = -\frac{t}{s}e^{-st} + \frac{1}{s}\int e^{-st}\,dt = -\frac{t}{s}e^{-st} - \frac{1}{s^2}e^{-st}. \]

Step 2 (Evaluate the improper integral).

\[ \int_0^\infty te^{-st}\,dt = \lim_{b\to\infty}\left(-\frac{b}{s}e^{-sb}-\frac{1}{s^2}e^{-sb}\right) - \left(0-\frac{1}{s^2}\right). \]

For \(s>0\), both \(be^{-sb}\) and \(e^{-sb}\) vanish as \(b\to\infty\) (the exponential decay beats the linear growth of \(b\)), so the limit is simply \(\dfrac{1}{s^2}\). Thus \(\mathcal{L}\{t\}=\dfrac{1}{s^2}\), and multiplying by \(2\) gives

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{2t\} = \frac{2}{s^2}, \qquad s>0.} \]

Problem 4 Hard

Prove directly from the definition (using integration by parts twice, the same technique used for \(\mathcal{L}\{\sin t\}\) in the notes) that \[ \mathcal{L}\{\cos(kt)\} = \frac{s}{s^2+k^2}, \qquad s>0, \] for any constant \(k\neq 0\).

Solution:

Let \(I=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\cos(kt)\,dt\). Because differentiating \(\cos\) or \(\sin\) never eliminates the trig function, a single integration by parts won't finish the job — instead, applying it twice will produce the original integral again, and we can then solve algebraically for it (exactly as in the \(\sin t\) example in the notes).

Step 1 (First integration by parts).

Let \(u=e^{-st}\), \(dv=\cos(kt)\,dt\), so \(du=-se^{-st}\,dt\), \(v=\frac{1}{k}\sin(kt)\):

\[ I = \left[\frac{1}{k}e^{-st}\sin(kt)\right]_0^\infty + \frac{s}{k}\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\sin(kt)\,dt. \]

The boundary term vanishes at both ends: at \(t=0\), \(\sin(0)=0\); as \(t\to\infty\), \(e^{-st}\to0\) (for \(s>0\)) while \(\sin(kt)\) stays bounded. So \(I=\dfrac{s}{k}J\), where \(J=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\sin(kt)\,dt\).

Step 2 (Second integration by parts).

Now apply the same idea to \(J\), with \(u=e^{-st}\), \(dv=\sin(kt)\,dt\), so \(du=-se^{-st}\,dt\), \(v=-\frac{1}{k}\cos(kt)\):

\[ J = \left[-\frac{1}{k}e^{-st}\cos(kt)\right]_0^\infty - \frac{s}{k}\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\cos(kt)\,dt. \]

At \(t\to\infty\) this boundary term again vanishes for \(s>0\); at \(t=0\) it equals \(-\frac{1}{k}(1)(1)=-\frac{1}{k}\). So the bracket contributes \(0-\left(-\frac1k\right)=\frac1k\), and

\[ J = \frac{1}{k} - \frac{s}{k}I. \]

Step 3 (Substitute back and solve for \(I\)).

Substituting this expression for \(J\) into \(I=\frac{s}{k}J\) brings back the original unknown \(I\):

\[ I = \frac{s}{k}\left(\frac{1}{k}-\frac{s}{k}I\right) = \frac{s}{k^2}-\frac{s^2}{k^2}I. \]

Moving the \(I\)-term on the right to the left side and factoring,

\[ I\left(1+\frac{s^2}{k^2}\right) = \frac{s}{k^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I\cdot\frac{k^2+s^2}{k^2}=\frac{s}{k^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = \frac{s}{s^2+k^2}. \]

So

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{\cos(kt)\} = \frac{s}{s^2+k^2}, \qquad s>0.} \]

Problem 5 Conceptual

Every transform computed in this section comes with a restriction like "\(s>0\)" or "\(s>a\)." Using the definition of the Laplace transform as an improper integral, explain why \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}\) requires \(s>a\) to exist, and what specifically goes wrong if \(s\le a\).

Solution:

By definition, \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}e^{at}\,dt =\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}\,dt\), which is exactly the same convergence question as the \(\mathcal{L}\{1\}\) computation in the notes, just with \(s\) replaced by \(s-a\).

Evaluating the antiderivative and taking the limit as the upper bound \(b\to\infty\) gives a term \(e^{-(s-a)b}\). This term tends to \(0\) exactly when the exponent \(-(s-a)b\) tends to \(-\infty\), which happens precisely when \(s-a>0\), i.e. \(s>a\). In that case the improper integral converges to the finite value \(\dfrac{1}{s-a}\).

If instead \(s\le a\), then \(s-a\le0\), so \(-(s-a)\ge0\) and \(e^{-(s-a)t}\) either grows without bound as \(t\to\infty\) (if \(s\lt a\)) or stays constantly equal to \(1\) forever (if \(s=a\)); either way, \(\int_0^b e^{-(s-a)t}\,dt\) does not settle down to a finite number as \(b\to\infty\) — the improper integral diverges, and \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}\}\) simply does not exist there. This is why every Laplace transform formula must be quoted together with the range of \(s\) for which it is actually valid: the formula itself may look fine algebraically for any \(s\), but the integral that defines it only converges on part of that range.

Problem 6 Easy

Compute \[ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{7s+12}{s^2+16}\right\}. \]

Solution:

The denominator \(s^2+16\) already matches the form \(s^2+k^2\) with \(k=4\), so once we split the numerator into two pieces, both terms will match table entries directly — no partial fractions needed here. Splitting,

\[ \frac{7s+12}{s^2+16} = 7\cdot\frac{s}{s^2+16} + 12\cdot\frac{1}{s^2+16}. \]

Using \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{s}{s^2+k^2}\right\}=\cos(kt)\) and \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{1}{s^2+k^2}\right\}=\dfrac{1}{k}\sin(kt)\) with \(k=4\), the first term gives \(7\cos(4t)\), and the second gives \(12\cdot\frac14\sin(4t)=3\sin(4t)\). So

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{7s+12}{s^2+16}\right\} = 7\cos(4t)+3\sin(4t).} \]

Problem 7 Easy

Compute \[ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{s}{s^3+4s}\right\}. \]

Solution:

Before reaching for partial fractions, it is always worth checking whether the expression simplifies first. Factoring an \(s\) out of the denominator,

\[ s^3+4s = s(s^2+4), \]

so the whole expression collapses by cancelling the common factor of \(s\):

\[ \frac{s}{s^3+4s} = \frac{s}{s(s^2+4)} = \frac{1}{s^2+4}. \]

This is now a direct table match, \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{1}{s^2+k^2}\right\}=\dfrac1k\sin(kt)\) with \(k=2\), so

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{s}{s^3+4s}\right\} = \frac{1}{2}\sin(2t).} \]

Problem 8 Medium

Compute \[ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{s-2}{s^2+3s+2}\right\}. \]

Solution:

Step 1 (Factor the denominator).

\[ s^2+3s+2=(s+1)(s+2). \]

Step 2 (Set up and solve the partial fraction decomposition).

Since the two roots are distinct and real, we decompose into two simple fractions:

\[ \frac{s-2}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{A}{s+1}+\frac{B}{s+2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s-2=A(s+2)+B(s+1). \]

Setting \(s=-1\): \(-3=A(1)\Rightarrow A=-3\). Setting \(s=-2\): \(-4=B(-1)\Rightarrow B=4\).

Step 3 (Take the inverse transform term by term).

Now that the quotient is rewritten as a sum, linearity of \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\) lets us transform each piece separately using \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac1{s-a}\right\}=e^{at}\):

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{s-2}{s^2+3s+2}\right\} = -3e^{-t}+4e^{-2t}.} \]

Problem 9 Hard

Compute \[ \mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{4s^2+9s-4}{s(s-1)(s+2)}\right\}. \]

Solution:

This is the same partial-fraction technique as before, just with a third distinct linear factor — the extra factor means an extra unknown constant to pin down, but the "plug in each root" trick still works one root at a time.

Step 1 (Set up the decomposition).

\[ \frac{4s^2+9s-4}{s(s-1)(s+2)} = \frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s-1}+\frac{C}{s+2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4s^2+9s-4 = A(s-1)(s+2)+Bs(s+2)+Cs(s-1). \]

Step 2 (Solve for each constant by plugging in the roots).

Each choice of \(s\) equal to one of the roots makes two of the three terms on the right vanish, isolating a single constant:

\[ s=0:\ -4=A(-1)(2)=-2A \Rightarrow A=2. \] \[ s=1:\ 4+9-4=9=B(1)(3)=3B \Rightarrow B=3. \] \[ s=-2:\ 16-18-4=-6=C(-2)(-3)=6C \Rightarrow C=-1. \]

Step 3 (Take the inverse transform term by term).

With the decomposition \(\dfrac{2}{s}+\dfrac{3}{s-1}-\dfrac{1}{s+2}\) in hand, each piece is a direct table entry:

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\frac{4s^2+9s-4}{s(s-1)(s+2)}\right\} = 2+3e^{t}-e^{-2t}.} \]

Problem 10 Hard

Solve the initial value problem \[ y''+9y = 10t+7, \qquad y(0)=0,\ y'(0)=0. \]

Solution:

Step 1 (Transform both sides).

Writing \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}\) and using the derivative formula with the given (zero) initial conditions, \(\mathcal{L}\{y''\}=s^2F(s)-sy(0)-y'(0)=s^2F(s)\). On the right, \(\mathcal{L}\{10t+7\}=\dfrac{10}{s^2}+\dfrac{7}{s}\). So

\[ s^2F(s)+9F(s) = \frac{10}{s^2}+\frac{7}{s}. \]

Step 2 (Solve for \(F(s)\)).

\[ (s^2+9)F(s) = \frac{10+7s}{s^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F(s) = \frac{7s+10}{s^2(s^2+9)}. \]

Step 3 (Partial fractions).

Since \(s^2\) is a repeated factor and \(s^2+9\) is irreducible over the reals, the decomposition needs four constants:

\[ \frac{7s+10}{s^2(s^2+9)} = \frac{A}{s}+\frac{B}{s^2}+\frac{Cs+D}{s^2+9} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 7s+10 = As(s^2+9)+B(s^2+9)+(Cs+D)s^2. \]

Expanding and collecting powers of \(s\) on the right gives \((A+C)s^3+(B+D)s^2+9As+9B\). Matching this to \(0\cdot s^3+0\cdot s^2+7s+10\) term by term: \(9A=7\Rightarrow A=\frac79\); \(9B=10\Rightarrow B=\frac{10}{9}\); \(A+C=0\Rightarrow C=-\frac79\); \(B+D=0\Rightarrow D=-\frac{10}{9}\). So

\[ F(s) = \frac{7}{9}\cdot\frac1s + \frac{10}{9}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2} - \frac{7}{9}\cdot\frac{s}{s^2+9} - \frac{10}{9}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2+9}. \]

Step 4 (Take the inverse transform).

Using \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{1/s\}=1\), \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{1/s^2\}=t\), \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{s/(s^2+9)\}=\cos(3t)\), and \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{1/(s^2+9)\}=\frac13\sin(3t)\) (so the last term contributes \(\frac{10}{9}\cdot\frac13=\frac{10}{27}\)),

\[ \boxed{y(t) = \frac{10}{9}t+\frac{7}{9}-\frac{7}{9}\cos(3t)-\frac{10}{27}\sin(3t).} \]

Problem 11 Conceptual

The Laplace transform (and its inverse) are linear operators. Explain why this linearity is not, by itself, enough to justify taking \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{6s+2}{s^2-1}\right\}\) by inverse-transforming the numerator and denominator separately. What job does partial fraction decomposition actually do here?

Solution:

Linearity of \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\) says that \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{c_1F(s)+c_2G(s)\} =c_1\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}+c_2\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{G(s)\}\) — it distributes over sums and scalar multiples, and only over sums and scalar multiples. There is no corresponding rule for products or quotients: in general \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)/G(s)\}\neq \mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F(s)\}/\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{G(s)\}\) (transforming a numerator and denominator independently and then dividing the resulting functions of \(t\) has no mathematical justification at all).

So a single quotient like \(\dfrac{6s+2}{s^2-1}\) cannot be inverse-transformed directly unless it happens to already match a table entry. Partial fraction decomposition rewrites this one quotient as a genuine sum of simpler quotients, \(\dfrac{4}{s-1}+\dfrac{2}{s+1}\), each of which matches a table entry on its own. Only once the expression is in this additive form does linearity legally apply, letting us transform each piece separately and add the results. In short: partial fractions doesn't make the inverse transform "linear" — it restates the problem in the one form (a sum) where linearity was already applicable.

Problem 12 Easy

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{te^{3t}\}. \]

Solution:

The function \(te^{3t}\) is exactly \(e^{at}f(t)\) with \(a=3\) and \(f(t)=t\), so this is a direct application of the First Translation Theorem, \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a)\), where \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}\).

Here \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{t\}=\dfrac{1}{s^2}\). Replacing every \(s\) in \(F(s)\) with \(s-3\) (the "shift" the theorem describes) gives

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{te^{3t}\} = \frac{1}{(s-3)^2}.} \]

Problem 13 Easy

Find \(\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}\), where \[ f(t)= \begin{cases} t^2, & 0 \le t \le 3 \\ t^2+1, & t>3. \end{cases} \]

Solution:

Whenever a piecewise function is really "one base formula, plus an extra piece that switches on at some point," the unit step function lets us combine it into a single expression instead of splitting the transform integral in two. Here the base formula \(t^2\) runs the whole time, and an extra constant \(1\) switches on at \(t=3\):

\[ f(t) = t^2 + \mathscr{U}(t-3). \]

(Check: for \(t<3\), \(\mathscr U(t-3)=0\) and \(f(t)=t^2\); for \(t\ge3\), \(\mathscr U(t-3)=1\) and \(f(t)=t^2+1\) — matching the given definition.)

Now transform each piece: \(\mathcal{L}\{t^2\}=\dfrac{2}{s^3}\) from the table, and \(\mathcal{L}\{\mathscr U(t-3)\}=\dfrac{e^{-3s}}{s}\) (the special case of the Second Translation Theorem where the "delayed function" is just the constant \(1\)). Adding these,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \frac{2}{s^3}+\frac{e^{-3s}}{s}.} \]

Problem 14 Medium

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{(t^2+2t)e^{t}\}. \]

Solution:

First split the function using linearity, so that the First Translation Theorem can be applied to each piece separately:

\[ (t^2+2t)e^t = t^2e^t + 2te^t. \]

From the table, \(\mathcal{L}\{t^2\}=\dfrac{2}{s^3}\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{t\}=\dfrac{1}{s^2}\). Applying the shift \(s\to s-1\) (since \(a=1\) here) to each:

\[ \mathcal{L}\{t^2e^t\} = \frac{2}{(s-1)^3}, \qquad \mathcal{L}\{2te^t\} = \frac{2}{(s-1)^2}. \]

Adding the two pieces together,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{(t^2+2t)e^{t}\} = \frac{2}{(s-1)^3}+\frac{2}{(s-1)^2}.} \]

Problem 15 Medium

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{(t-1)\mathscr{U}(t-2)\}. \]

Solution:

The Second Translation Theorem, \(\mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathscr U(t-a)\}=e^{-as}F(s)\), applies to a function of \(t-a\) multiplying \(\mathscr U(t-a)\) — but here the factor multiplying \(\mathscr U(t-2)\) is \(t-1\), not \(t-2\). So the first step is purely algebraic: rewrite \(t-1\) in terms of \((t-2)\).

Step 1 (Rewrite in terms of \(t-2\)).

\[ t-1 = (t-2)+1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (t-1)\mathscr U(t-2) = (t-2)\mathscr U(t-2) + \mathscr U(t-2). \]

Step 2 (Apply the Second Translation Theorem to each piece).

The first piece has \(f(t)=t\) shifted by \(a=2\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{(t-2)\mathscr U(t-2)\}=e^{-2s}\mathcal{L}\{t\}=e^{-2s}\cdot\dfrac1{s^2}\). The second piece is the constant \(1\) shifted by \(a=2\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{\mathscr U(t-2)\}=e^{-2s}\cdot\dfrac1s\).

Adding the two pieces,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{(t-1)\mathscr{U}(t-2)\} = e^{-2s}\left(\frac{1}{s^2}+\frac{1}{s}\right).} \]

Problem 16 Hard

Solve the initial value problem \[ y''-5y'+6y = e^t\cos t, \qquad y(0)=1,\ y'(0)=0. \]

Solution:

Step 1 (Transform both sides).

With \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{y(t)\}\) and the given initial conditions, \(\mathcal{L}\{y''\}=s^2F(s)-s\) and \(\mathcal{L}\{y'\}=sF(s)-1\). On the right, the forcing term needs the First Translation Theorem: since \(\mathcal{L}\{\cos t\}=\dfrac{s}{s^2+1}\), shifting by \(a=1\) gives \(\mathcal{L}\{e^t\cos t\}=\dfrac{s-1}{(s-1)^2+1}\). So

\[ \big(s^2F(s)-s\big) - 5\big(sF(s)-1\big)+6F(s) = \frac{s-1}{(s-1)^2+1}. \]

Step 2 (Solve for \(F(s)\)).

\[ (s^2-5s+6)F(s) = \frac{s-1}{(s-1)^2+1}+s-5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F(s) = \underbrace{\frac{s-1}{(s-2)(s-3)\big[(s-1)^2+1\big]}}_{\text{piece 1}} + \underbrace{\frac{s-5}{(s-2)(s-3)}}_{\text{piece 2}}. \]

Step 3 (Partial fractions on piece 2).

\[ \frac{s-5}{(s-2)(s-3)} = \frac{A}{s-2}+\frac{B}{s-3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s-5=A(s-3)+B(s-2). \]

Setting \(s=2\): \(-3=-A\Rightarrow A=3\). Setting \(s=3\): \(-2=B\Rightarrow B=-2\). So piece 2 is \(\dfrac{3}{s-2}-\dfrac{2}{s-3}\).

Step 4 (Partial fractions on piece 1).

Since \((s-1)^2+1\) is an irreducible quadratic, piece 1 needs the form

\[ \frac{s-1}{(s-2)(s-3)\big[(s-1)^2+1\big]} = \frac{A'}{s-2}+\frac{B'}{s-3}+\frac{Cs+D}{(s-1)^2+1}. \]

Plugging in \(s=2\) and \(s=3\) isolates \(A'\) and \(B'\) just as before (the quadratic factor doesn't vanish at either root, so it just comes along for the ride):

\[ s=2:\ 1 = A'(-1)\big[(1)^2+1\big] = -2A' \Rightarrow A'=-\tfrac12. \] \[ s=3:\ 2 = B'(1)\big[(2)^2+1\big] = 5B' \Rightarrow B'=\tfrac25. \]

Matching the remaining coefficients (comparing the \(s^3\), \(s^2\), and constant terms after clearing denominators) gives \(C=\tfrac{1}{10}\), \(D=-\tfrac25\) — so piece 1 is \(\dfrac{-1/2}{s-2}+\dfrac{2/5}{s-3}+\dfrac{(1/10)s-2/5}{(s-1)^2+1}\).

Step 5 (Combine both pieces).

Adding piece 1 and piece 2 term by term: the \(1/(s-2)\) coefficients give \(3-\tfrac12=\tfrac52\); the \(1/(s-3)\) coefficients give \(-2+\tfrac25=-\tfrac85\). So

\[ F(s) = \frac{5/2}{s-2} - \frac{8/5}{s-3} + \frac{(1/10)s-2/5}{(s-1)^2+1}. \]

Step 6 (Take the inverse transform).

For the last term, write the numerator in terms of \((s-1)\) so it matches the shifted table entries: \(\frac{1}{10}s-\frac25 = \frac{1}{10}(s-1)-\frac{3}{10}\). Then, using \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{s-1}{(s-1)^2+1}\right\}=e^t\cos t\) and \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\dfrac{1}{(s-1)^2+1}\right\}=e^t\sin t\) (both from the First Translation Theorem in reverse),

\[ \boxed{y(t) = \frac{5}{2}e^{2t} - \frac{8}{5}e^{3t} + \frac{1}{10}e^{t}\cos t - \frac{3}{10}e^{t}\sin t.} \]

(As a check, \(y(0)=\frac52-\frac85+\frac1{10}=\frac{25-16+1}{10}=1\), matching the initial condition.)

Problem 17 Hard

Solve the initial value problem \(y'+3y=f(t)\), \(y(0)=0\), where \[ f(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0\le t<2 \\ 6 & t\ge2 \end{cases} = 6\,\mathscr{U}(t-2). \]

Solution:

Step 1 (Transform both sides).

With \(y(0)=0\), \(\mathcal{L}\{y'\}=sF(s)\). On the right, \(\mathcal{L}\{\mathscr U(t-2)\}=\dfrac{e^{-2s}}{s}\), so

\[ sF(s)+3F(s) = 6\cdot\frac{e^{-2s}}{s}. \]

Step 2 (Solve for \(F(s)\)).

\[ F(s) = \frac{6e^{-2s}}{s(s+3)}. \]

Step 3 (Partial fractions on the non-shifted part).

Setting aside the \(e^{-2s}\) factor for a moment, decompose \(\dfrac{6}{s(s+3)}=\dfrac As+\dfrac{B}{s+3}\): \(6=A(s+3)+Bs\). At \(s=0\): \(A=2\). At \(s=-3\): \(-3B=6\Rightarrow B=-2\). So

\[ F(s) = e^{-2s}\left(\frac{2}{s}-\frac{2}{s+3}\right). \]

Step 4 (Apply the Second Translation Theorem).

Let \(G(s)=\dfrac2s-\dfrac2{s+3}\), so \(g(t)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{G(s)\}=2-2e^{-3t}\). By the Second Translation Theorem, \(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{e^{-2s}G(s)\}=g(t-2)\mathscr U(t-2)\), i.e. we substitute \(t-2\) in place of \(t\) everywhere in \(g\) and multiply by \(\mathscr U(t-2)\):

\[ \boxed{y(t) = \Big(2-2e^{-3(t-2)}\Big)\mathscr{U}(t-2).} \]

This says the tank/system stays at rest (\(y=0\)) until \(t=2\), exactly matching \(y(0)=0\) and the fact that the forcing term is off until then, and only afterward rises toward the value \(2\).

Problem 18 Conceptual

The First Translation Theorem says that multiplying \(f(t)\) by \(e^{at}\) before transforming produces the shift \(\mathcal{L}\{e^{at}f(t)\}=F(s-a)\). The Second Translation Theorem says that delaying \(f(t)\) by \(a\) units and switching it on at \(t=a\) produces \(\mathcal{L}\{f(t-a)\mathscr{U}(t-a)\}=e^{-as}F(s)\). In what sense are these two theorems "mirror images" of each other?

Solution:

Each theorem takes an operation performed in one domain (\(t\) or \(s\)) and shows that it corresponds to the other kind of operation in the opposite domain — and the two theorems swap which operation goes with which domain.

In the First Translation Theorem, we multiply by an exponential \(e^{at}\) in the \(t\)-domain (before ever transforming), and the effect on the transform side is a shift: every \(s\) in \(F(s)\) gets replaced by \(s-a\). In the Second Translation Theorem, it is exactly the other way around: we shift \(f\) in the \(t\)-domain (delaying it by \(a\) and switching it on with \(\mathscr U(t-a)\)), and the effect on the transform side is to multiply \(F(s)\) by the exponential \(e^{-as}\).

So multiplication-by-an-exponential in one domain always pairs with a shift in the other domain, and which domain plays which role simply flips between the two theorems: "exponential in \(t\) \(\Rightarrow\) shift in \(s\)" versus "shift in \(t\) \(\Rightarrow\) exponential in \(s\)." Recognizing this duality is often the fastest way to tell, at a glance, which theorem a given problem calls for.

Problem 19 Easy

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{t\sin(2t)\}. \]

Solution:

This is a direct application of the derivative-of-transform theorem with \(n=1\): \(\mathcal{L}\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s)\), where \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\}\).

Here \(f(t)=\sin(2t)\), so \(F(s)=\dfrac{2}{s^2+4}\). Differentiating with the chain rule,

\[ F'(s) = 2\cdot\frac{-2s}{(s^2+4)^2} = \frac{-4s}{(s^2+4)^2}. \]

Multiplying by \(-1\) as the theorem requires,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{t\sin(2t)\} = \frac{4s}{(s^2+4)^2}.} \]

Problem 20 Medium

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{te^{-2t}\cos(5t)\}. \]

Solution:

This problem stacks two tools from this unit: the First Translation Theorem handles the \(e^{-2t}\) factor, and then the derivative-of-transform theorem handles the extra \(t\).

Step 1 (Apply the First Translation Theorem).

Start from \(\mathcal{L}\{\cos(5t)\}=\dfrac{s}{s^2+25}\). Shifting by \(a=-2\) (i.e. replacing \(s\) with \(s-(-2)=s+2\)) gives the transform of \(e^{-2t}\cos(5t)\) itself:

\[ F(s) = \mathcal{L}\{e^{-2t}\cos(5t)\} = \frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+25}. \]

Step 2 (Apply the derivative-of-transform theorem).

Now \(\mathcal{L}\{t\cdot[e^{-2t}\cos(5t)]\}=-F'(s)\). Writing \(u=s+2\) so \(F=\dfrac{u}{u^2+25}\) and \(\frac{d}{ds}=\frac{d}{du}\) (since \(du/ds=1\)), the quotient rule gives

\[ F'(s) = \frac{(u^2+25)-u(2u)}{(u^2+25)^2} = \frac{25-u^2}{(u^2+25)^2} = \frac{25-(s+2)^2}{\big((s+2)^2+25\big)^2}. \]

Multiplying by \(-1\),

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{te^{-2t}\cos(5t)\} = \frac{(s+2)^2-25}{\big((s+2)^2+25\big)^2}.} \]

Problem 21 Hard

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{t^2\sin(2t)\}. \]

Solution:

Here \(n=2\) in the derivative-of-transform theorem, \(\mathcal{L}\{t^2f(t)\}=F''(s)\) (recall \((-1)^2=1\)), so this problem takes the differentiation from Problem 19 one step further.

Starting from \(F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{\sin(2t)\}=\dfrac{2}{s^2+4}\), the first derivative (computed the same way as in Problem 19) is

\[ F'(s) = \frac{-4s}{(s^2+4)^2}. \]

Differentiate once more, using the quotient rule with numerator \(-4s\) and denominator \((s^2+4)^2\) (whose derivative is \(2(s^2+4)(2s)=4s(s^2+4)\)):

\[ F''(s) = \frac{-4(s^2+4)^2 - (-4s)\cdot 4s(s^2+4)}{(s^2+4)^4} = \frac{(s^2+4)\big[-4(s^2+4)+16s^2\big]}{(s^2+4)^4} = \frac{12s^2-16}{(s^2+4)^3}. \]

Since the theorem calls for \((-1)^2F''(s)=F''(s)\) with no sign change,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{t^2\sin(2t)\} = \frac{12s^2-16}{(s^2+4)^3}.} \]

Problem 22 Hard

Find \[ \mathcal{L}\{t^2 e^{2t}\sin(3t)\}. \]

Solution:

This combines all three tools at once: the First Translation Theorem for the \(e^{2t}\) factor, followed by two derivatives of the resulting transform for the \(t^2\) factor.

Step 1 (Apply the First Translation Theorem).

From \(\mathcal{L}\{\sin(3t)\}=\dfrac{3}{s^2+9}\), shifting by \(a=2\) gives the transform of \(e^{2t}\sin(3t)\):

\[ F(s) = \frac{3}{(s-2)^2+9}. \]

Step 2 (Differentiate twice).

Let \(u=s-2\), so \(F=\dfrac{3}{u^2+9}\) and derivatives in \(s\) equal derivatives in \(u\). First,

\[ F'(s) = 3\cdot\frac{-2u}{(u^2+9)^2} = \frac{-6u}{(u^2+9)^2}. \]

Differentiating again with the quotient rule (numerator \(-6u\), denominator \((u^2+9)^2\), whose derivative is \(4u(u^2+9)\)):

\[ F''(s) = \frac{-6(u^2+9)^2 - (-6u)(4u)(u^2+9)}{(u^2+9)^4} = \frac{(u^2+9)\big[-6(u^2+9)+24u^2\big]}{(u^2+9)^4} = \frac{18u^2-54}{(u^2+9)^3}. \]

Step 3 (Substitute back and apply the sign).

Since \((-1)^2=1\), \(\mathcal{L}\{t^2e^{2t}\sin(3t)\}=F''(s)\); substituting \(u=s-2\) back in,

\[ \boxed{\mathcal{L}\{t^2 e^{2t}\sin(3t)\} = \frac{18(s-2)^2-54}{\big((s-2)^2+9\big)^3}.} \]

Problem 23 Conceptual

The theorem in this section states \(\mathcal{L}\{t^nf(t)\} = (-1)^n\dfrac{d^n}{ds^n}F(s)\). Without computing any specific transform, explain where the alternating sign \((-1)^n\) comes from, and why it is \((-1)^n\) rather than the transform simply equaling \(F^{(n)}(s)\) outright.

Solution:

The sign traces back to a single derivative: since \(F(s)=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt\), differentiating under the integral sign with respect to \(s\) brings down one factor of \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s}(e^{-st})=-te^{-st}\) — that is, each \(s\)-derivative multiplies the integrand by an extra factor of \(-t\), not \(t\).

Differentiating once gives \(F'(s)=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty(-t)e^{-st}f(t)\,dt =-\mathcal{L}\{tf(t)\}\), so \(\mathcal{L}\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s)\), matching \(n=1\) in the theorem (\((-1)^1=-1\)). Differentiating a second time brings down another factor of \(-t\), so the integrand now carries \((-t)^2=t^2\) — the two minus signs cancel — giving \(F''(s)=\mathcal{L}\{t^2f(t)\}\), matching \(n=2\) (\((-1)^2=1\)).

In general, differentiating \(n\) times brings down \(n\) factors of \(-t\), i.e. a factor of \((-t)^n=(-1)^nt^n\), so \(F^{(n)}(s)=\mathcal{L}\{(-1)^nt^nf(t)\} =(-1)^n\mathcal{L}\{t^nf(t)\}\). Multiplying both sides by \((-1)^n\) and using \(\big((-1)^n\big)^2=1\) gives \(\mathcal{L}\{t^nf(t)\}=(-1)^nF^{(n)}(s)\) — exactly the theorem. The sign is not a separate fact to memorize; it is forced by the chain rule applied to \(e^{-st}\), and it alternates because each differentiation contributes its own factor of \(-1\) that either matches or cancels the ones already there depending on whether \(n\) is odd or even.
